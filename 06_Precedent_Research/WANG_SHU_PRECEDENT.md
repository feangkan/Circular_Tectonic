## Overview

Wang Shu, 2012 Pritzker Prize laureate, is known for large-scale reuse of reclaimed building materials — most famously roof tiles and bricks salvaged from demolished traditional houses across Zhejiang province — assembled using a traditional technique known as **"Wa Pan"** (tile/wall assembly), most visibly at the **Ningbo History Museum** (2008).

## Key Strategies

- Salvaged material sourced in bulk from demolition sites ahead of construction, not purchased as new stock.
- Reclaimed tiles/bricks are inherently non-uniform (different sizes, colours, wear conditions) — treated as a design asset, not a defect to hide.
- Traditional craft assembly logic (Wa Pan wall construction) reused and scaled up to contemporary building size.
- The resulting facades read as **textured, banded, or gradient-like** surfaces — variation is organised, not random, even though individual units are irregular.

## Material/Tectonic System

Reclaimed tile/brick units are laid up in walls using traditional wall-building logic, adapted to modern structural backing. The irregularity of each salvaged piece is retained and made visible; ordering comes from how units are grouped and coursed, not from making the units uniform.

## Lessons for Our Research

- Directly relevant to the tutor's four directives on our own project: **the model is "irregular units + deliberate coursing/ordering," not "irregular units + random placement."**
- Supports the field-driven aggregation approach already outlined in `01_Weekly_Tasks/Week_04_ShellFormFinding/02_Computational_Strategy.md` — Wang Shu achieves the ordering by hand/craft judgement; our equivalent is a computational field (sun exposure/height/stress) driving panel overlap and finish selection systematically.
- Reinforces that weathering/condition variation (rust, patina) can be a legible, intentional surface language rather than something to normalise away — directly answers the "rust or treated" and "organise colour/weathering" directives.
