# MAIN PRECEDENT — Thin-Shell Riveted Aluminium/Metal Pavilions

**Status: this is now the project's primary precedent cluster**, per direction to keep Thin-Shell Metal Woven Pavilion (XISUI Design) as the main precedent, supported by two closely related projects by MARC FORNES / THEVERYMANY that the pavilion precedent study independently converged on. All three share the same underlying fabrication logic — thin metal sheet, computationally segmented into stripes/panels, riveted or bolted (never welded) into a self-supporting double-curved shell — which is why they're grouped together here rather than treated as three separate precedents. Written in slide-ready blocks: each project has a stats block, a concept block, and a fabrication block that can be lifted directly into a presentation slide.

## PROJECT 1 (MAIN) — Thin-Shell Nest Pavilion / "Thin-Shell Metal Woven Pavilion"

**Designer:** XISUI Design
**Location:** Sited over a pool (China)
**Timeline:** First assembled 2020 → relocated and reconstructed 2022 → finally completed 2023
**Span:** 11m × 8m

### Key Stats (slide-ready)
| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Material | Metal plate, 2 layers, 2.5mm thick each |
| Span-to-thickness ratio | 4,400 : 1 |
| Structural connection | Bolts only — welding-free construction |
| Load basis | Calculated against wind loads and gravity loads |
| Lifecycle | Built once, fully disassembled, relocated, rebuilt (2020→2022→2023) |

### Design Concept
<cite index="110-1">The design imitates the nesting behaviour of birds in nature with a "keel-free" design — a curvy structure where only a thin surface is needed for the load-bearing task, rather than a separate structural skeleton beneath a skin.</cite> <cite index="110-1">Its structural mechanics are explicitly compared to two precedents: vaulted buildings from ancient construction, and the eggshells of birds in nature</cite> — i.e. the same double-curvature-equals-strength principle in both a historic human construction tradition and a natural structural system.

### Fabrication Strategy
<cite index="110-1">This is a welding-free construction — from the foundation to the main volume, bolts are used for point-to-point seams instead.</cite> <cite index="110-1">Each bolt is digitally designed and verified in advance specifically to avoid the form-finding errors that come with a conventional manual welding process</cite> — meaning the fabrication method was chosen *because* it let the computational design be trusted to build accurately, not just because it's reversible. <cite index="110-1">The direct payoff of choosing bolts over welding: parts, or even the entire structure, can be replaced or relocated — which is exactly what happened when the whole pavilion was disassembled and rebuilt at a new site two years after first being built.</cite>

### Spatial/Experiential Notes (useful for slide narrative)
<cite index="110-1">Visitors pass through a sunken passage below the pool, bringing human perception closer to the water surface. The exterior is white to reflect off the water; the interior is matt brown-gold, keeping brightness and reflection low for an immersive, cave-like, contemplative feeling.</cite> <cite index="110-1">Openings between the dual layers let dynamic patterns of light move across the interior dome as the sun and clouds change through the day.</cite>

## PROJECT 2 — Zephyr Pavilion, Texas Tech University (MARC FORNES / THEVERYMANY)

**Client:** Texas Tech University, Lubbock, TX
**Design + Computation:** MARC FORNES / THEVERYMANY
**Engineering:** CRAFT Engineering Studio
**Photography:** NAARO

### Key Stats (slide-ready)
| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Dimensions | 18' H × 48' W × 13' D |
| Material | Two layers of 3mm aluminium |
| Total linear cut distance | 7,400 metres |
| Number of parts | 2,343 |
| Number of rivets | 59,216 |

### Design Concept
<cite index="98-1">Sited in the courtyard of the Honors Residence Hall, Zephyr acts as a hybrid interior/exterior space along an active campus path — sweeping cantilevers offer shade between two "loop columns" that mirror each other, drawing topographical lines into smooth volumetric rings that open to the sky at a knot-like centre.</cite> <cite index="98-1">The columns expand into branching "funneled bridges," giving the overarching canopy strong points of rest, while a soft yet intricate network builds the foundation for self-supporting cantilevered wings.</cite>

### Fabrication Strategy — "Structural Stripes"
<cite index="108-1">THEVERYMANY's building system, developed and refined across a decade of projects, is called "Structural Stripes" — custom-designed parts form complex, self-supporting curvilinear surfaces without a separate structural skeleton; the studio's body of work is collectively known as "Crawling Assemblies."</cite> <cite index="98-1">At Zephyr specifically, the overall form takes shape by assembling one stripe-part at a time to its neighbour, in a very precise sequence — a part forced into place out of sequence simply will not work, so timing/sequence is itself a primary driver of how the surface takes shape.</cite> <cite index="98-1">Each stripe is rotated 45 degrees to the vertical span so it simultaneously carries load AND acts as the connection point for the next part — this is what allows the material to stay as thin as it does. Controlled folds of 6–15 degrees per stripe produce tangential continuity of curvature, giving the form its relaxed, organic-reading movement despite being built from flat, angular stock.</cite> <cite index="100-1">By modelling geometries computationally, the studio devised a method where structure and skin become a single piece — the entire pavilion is able to freely support itself with no added structural frame.</cite>

## PROJECT 3 — The Orb, Google Charleston East Campus (MARC FORNES / THEVERYMANY)

**Client:** Google
**Design + Computation:** MARC FORNES / THEVERYMANY
**Location:** Mountain View, California

### Key Stats (slide-ready)
| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Height | 10m / 33' |
| Material | 3mm aluminium |
| Total linear cut distance | 26,820 metres |
| Number of parts | 6,441 |
| Number of rivets | 217,847 |

### Design Concept
<cite index="99-1">A centrepiece for Google's Charleston East Campus public plaza, The Orb is an ultra-thin, self-supported aluminium shell whose surface is punctuated by a pattern of openings that filter daylight into a dappled ground pattern by day and glow as a lit sculpture by night.</cite> <cite index="107-1">Described by the studio as "edged yet edgeless" — surfaces curve, branch, split, rejoin, and split again — this extreme curvature is what allows the surface to be entirely self-supporting despite being just 3mm thick.</cite>

### Fabrication Strategy
Identical underlying logic to Zephyr — <cite index="107-1">geometric panels are riveted together into an undulating, doubly curved sphere, using cutting-edge computational design to make the extreme curvature achievable at such minimal thickness.</cite> The scale jump from Zephyr (2,343 parts / 59,216 rivets) to The Orb (6,441 parts / 217,847 rivets) is itself a useful data point for the presentation — it shows the Structural Stripes/aggregation method is genuinely scalable, not a small-object-only technique.

## Why These Three Belong Together (the case for treating them as one precedent cluster)

| | Thin-Shell Nest Pavilion (Main) | Zephyr Pavilion | The Orb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material | 2× 2.5mm metal plate | 2× 3mm aluminium | 3mm aluminium |
| Connection | Bolts (welding-free) | Rivets | Rivets |
| Biomimetic reference | Bird nesting behaviour + eggshell mechanics | Not explicitly biomimetic — organic reading is a byproduct of the stripe system | Organic branching form, not explicitly biomimetic in the source text |
| Structural principle | Double curvature = load path, no separate skeleton | Structure and skin are a single piece | Extreme curvature enables self-support at 3mm |
| Disassembly demonstrated | Yes — actually relocated and rebuilt | Not stated | Not stated |
| Scale | 11m × 8m span | 18'×48'×13' | 10m tall, 26.8km of cut edge |

**The core transferable lesson across all three:** none of these projects treat "structure" and "skin" as separate systems — the thinness and the strength come from the same double-curved geometric move, and the fabrication method (bolt or rivet, never weld) is chosen specifically because it's the only way to trust a computationally-precise thin-shell geometry to actually assemble correctly on site. This directly validates this project's own biomorphic growth-algorithm computational lead (`07_Computational_Design/BIOMORPHIC_GROWTH_ALGORITHMS.md`) — a growth-generated geometry, like these precedents' segmented stripe patterns, produces a network of small variable panels that need exactly this kind of joint (a mechanical, precisely-located, non-thermal connection) to build reliably.

## Direct Implications for This Project's Pavilion (for the presentation's "lessons applied" section)

1. **The metal-sheet material research (`RECLAIMED_METAL_SCRAP.md`, List C) is directly reinforced** — thin sheet stock, not thick sectional stock, is what makes this fabrication logic possible. Reclaimed roofing sheet, ACP offcuts, or stainless kitchen offcuts (all already in the catalogue) are viable stock for this approach.
2. **Rivet/bolt is now the clearly preferred connection method for the skin layer** (over weld) — consistent with the fabrication methods document's own recommendation, now backed by three real built precedents at real scale.
3. **The growth algorithm's output should be thought of as a stripe/panel segmentation problem**, not just a node-network problem — differential growth would need to generate a *paneling pattern* across a target surface (much like Zephyr's 45°-rotated stripe logic) rather than only a branching line network, if this precedent cluster is going to be genuinely followed rather than just referenced.
4. **Disassembly is provable, not just theoretical** — the Nest Pavilion's actual relocation is strong evidence for the studio's design-for-disassembly assessment criterion, more convincing than any of this project's other precedents that only claim disassembly is possible in principle.

## Addendum — ICD/ITKE Research Pavilions Checked: No Direct Material Match, Three Partial Matches Worth Noting

Checked against https://www.itke.uni-stuttgart.de/research/icd-itke-research-pavilions/ specifically for metal/GFRC precedents. **Honest finding: none of ITKE's numbered Research Pavilions (2010–2025) use metal or true GFRC** — their whole research programme centres on fibre-composite (carbon/glass FRP filament winding) and timber/bamboo. Three adjacent projects are worth citing separately rather than folding into the main precedent above, since they don't share this project's exact material palette:

**Elytra Filament Pavilion (Vitra Campus / V&A London, 2016):** <cite index="112-1">A 200m² bionic canopy of 40 robotically-wound hexagonal glass/carbon-fibre composite cells, each cell's dense fibre pattern a direct result of structural simulation carried out in advance, weighing under 9kg/m² — explicitly modelled on the lightweight fibrous structure of beetle forewing shells (elytra).</cite> Fibre composite rather than metal/GFRC, but its steel/cable support partners (Stahlbau Wendeler, PFEIFER Seil-und Hebetechnik) and biomimetic cellular-module logic are adjacent to this project's direction. https://www.itke.uni-stuttgart.de/research/built-projects/elytra-filament-pavilion-vitra-campus/

**Spatially Branched, Braided Structures for Concrete-Filled Structural FRP Nodes (current research, 2023–2026):** The closest material match found. <cite index="114-1">A braided fibre-reinforced-polymer (FRP) hull filled with a concrete core, explicitly proposed as an alternative to steel structural nodes — steel nodes being energy-intensive and costly to manufacture, whereas the FRP hull serves as both reinforcement for the concrete and lost formwork, with far greater geometric freedom for complex branching shapes.</cite> Not GFRC exactly (a wrapped hull + concrete core, not fibre dispersed through a concrete matrix), but conceptually the nearest ITKE gets to this project's metal-alternative + concrete-composite + branching-node interests. https://www.itke.uni-stuttgart.de/research/current-research-projects/spatially-branched-braided-structures-for-concrete-filled-structural-frp-nodes/

**Reclaimed Design (ITECH M.Sc. thesis, 2024):** Wrong material (reclaimed lumber, not metal) but methodologically identical to this project's grading-and-assignment approach. <cite index="113-1">An availability-oriented design framework where machine learning predicts compressive strength from non-destructive tests on reclaimed lumber, generating a high-resolution material property gradient that a 7m demonstration truss is then designed against.</cite> Same underlying lesson as the EPFL Ski Pavilion (Chain 5 in `09_Systemic_Design_Framework/MATERIAL_FABRICATION_PRECEDENT_SYSTEM_MAP.md`), now backed by an ML-based grading method rather than manual sorting — worth citing as evidence the "grade then assign" strategy scales up in sophistication. https://www.itke.uni-stuttgart.de/teaching/past-student-projects/reclaimed-design/

**Conclusion:** the Thin-Shell Nest Pavilion / Zephyr / The Orb cluster above remains the strongest and most directly applicable precedent set for this project's actual material palette (thin metal sheet, riveted/bolted, biomimetic double curvature) — ITKE's programme, while the most rigorous biomimetic-computational research body reviewed all semester, simply works in a different material family (fibre composite, timber) and shouldn't be forced into a false material match.
