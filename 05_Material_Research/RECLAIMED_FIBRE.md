# Fibre Study — Reclaimed Fibre & Precedents

A fourth material family, alongside metal, GFRC, and wood: reclaimed *fibre* as a primary material in its own right — rope, netting, and textile waste — rather than only as an additive inside a concrete matrix (that question was already covered separately in `CIRCULAR_FIBRE_FOR_GFRC.md`). This document treats fibre as something that can be woven, netted, or pressed into panels and used structurally or as skin, on its own terms.

---

## Three Reclaimed Fibre Streams

### 1. Reclaimed Rope & Cordage

**Source:** Marine/shipping industry — rope lost or discarded at sea, dockyard and fishing fleet offcuts, decommissioned rigging.

**Scale of the waste stream:** <cite index="8-1">More than 640,000 tonnes of rope are lost or discarded irregularly at sea by ships and boats worldwide every year — equivalent to nearly 3.3% of all rope market production.</cite> This is a genuinely large, continuous, global waste stream, comparable in scale to some of the metal and timber streams already researched.

**Physical characteristics:** High-strength synthetic fibre (typically nylon, polyamide, or HDPE), naturally suited to tension, often already weathered/salt-exposed, comes in long continuous lengths rather than fixed panel sizes.

**Structural behaviour — the key insight:** rope is a tension material by default. The interesting design move, seen directly in the precedent below, is applying biomimetic principles to make a tension-optimised material perform well in *compression* instead, by bundling/coiling it into a dense, brick-like module.

### 2. Reclaimed Fishing Net

**Source:** "Ghost nets" (lost/abandoned fishing gear) and end-of-life commercial netting, collected via marine cleanup and fisheries recycling programs.

**Physical characteristics:** Open woven/knotted mesh, typically nylon or HDPE, inherently flexible and drapable — closer to a fabric than a rope in how it can be worked.

**Scale of the waste stream:** <cite index="7-1">Derelict fishing gear causes an estimated 380,000 marine animal deaths annually; roughly 5.7% of fishing nets, 8.6% of traps, and 29% of lines from the global fishing industry were lost into the ocean in a single year (2017 estimate).</cite> This is as much an environmental remediation opportunity as a material sourcing one — using reclaimed fishing net is genuinely removing active marine hazard, not just diverting landfill waste.

**Existing recycling infrastructure — a real advantage over most reclaimed materials researched this semester:** several manufacturers already process waste fishing net into consistent, quality-controlled textile yarn — <cite index="5-1">REFINVERSE (Japan) collects and recycles used fishing nets from Hokkaido and elsewhere, cleaning and processing them into REAMIDE pellets for textile fibre production, later used in projects like Okamura's Renet upholstery fabric.</cite> <cite index="6-1">Taiwan-based manufacturers produce MARINYLON, a recycled nylon fabric made from retired fishing nets, maintaining consistent quality suitable for demanding technical applications like footwear.</cite> This means, unlike reclaimed GFRC or reclaimed timber, fishing net has an *established commercial processing pathway* already converting raw waste into a usable, quality-controlled material — a genuinely lower-risk sourcing proposition.

### 3. Reclaimed Textile Waste (denim/cotton, general post-consumer textile)

**Source:** Post-consumer clothing waste, denim manufacturing offcuts, garment industry production waste.

**Scale of the waste stream:** <cite index="13-1">The EPA estimated in 2018 that only 13% of clothing textile waste is recycled, with landfills receiving 11.3 million tons of textiles — 7.7% of all municipal solid waste landfilled that year.</cite> This is a genuinely enormous, under-recycled stream.

**Physical characteristics/performance:** <cite index="14-1">Composite panels made of 100% waste wool fibre showed thermal conductivity between 0.049–0.060 W/(m·K), comparable to conventional building insulation materials.</cite> <cite index="12-1">Cotton denim and polyester selvedge waste can be converted into fibres, carded, and hot-pressed into sandwich-structure insulation panels.</cite> <cite index="11-1">Composite panels combining waste denim fibre (reinforcement) with recycled polypropylene/polyethylene packaging waste (matrix) achieved sound transmission loss up to 8dB and thermal resistance up to 0.11 m²·°C/W.</cite>

**Honest limitation:** <cite index="10-1">Recycled denim insulation carries higher embodied carbon per unit mass than some alternatives despite lower production energy — the environmental case rests more on landfill diversion than on being unambiguously the lowest-carbon option available.</cite> <cite index="15-1">It's also currently 10–50% more expensive than fiberglass, due to smaller production volumes.</cite> Worth being upfront about this rather than overselling textile waste as a clear-cut winner.

## Precedent 1 — Recycled Rope Pavilion (Leinemann | Ortiz, 2019)

**Status:** Unbuilt design proposal, New York, USA (Roosevelt Island)

**Concept:** <cite index="9-1">By applying concepts of biomimetics, a rope — usually used in tension — is made to perform optimally in compression instead, formed into a brick-like module that can be easily assembled and transported.</cite> <cite index="9-1">Two structural layers of rope modules anchor a main nautical-fabric canopy and a secondary mesh layer; loose ropes hang from the ceiling creating a playful, tactile interior space that invites touching and swinging.</cite> <cite index="9-1">3D-printed modules and 1:1 mock-ups were used as study tools to understand the geometry and form before committing to final design.</cite>

**Why this is the standout precedent for this project:** this is a *biomimetics-first* pavilion — exactly the same design logic this project's growth-algorithm/natural-mimicry lead is built around — applied to a genuinely reclaimed fibre stream at real architectural scale (not just a lab sample). The tension-to-compression reversal is also a direct, concrete example of "material behaviour driving form," the same principle already established across the metal, GFRC, and timber research this semester.

## Precedent 2 — ¡Qué Faena(r)! (+cruz.atelier, Galicia, Spain)

**Status:** Built, circular pavilion

**Concept:** <cite index="3-1">A pavilion inspired by Galicia's mussel-platform structures, built from two recognisable elements: the traditional mussel platform as the main structure, and the traditional fishing system (nets) on its roof as a lure and shelter for activities underneath — over 82% of its form constructed from materials that had reached the end of their useful life, including fishing nets, rusty drums, reclaimed mussel-platform wood, and granite blocks.</cite> <cite index="3-1">The project explicitly addresses circular economy principles while integrating traditional craftsmanship and sustainable practice, becoming a mediator between the urban, cultural, and traditional elements of the place.</cite>

**Why this matters:** a genuinely built (not proposal-stage) example of fishing net used architecturally alongside reclaimed wood and metal — directly validating a *multi-material* pavilion approach (net + wood + metal drums) rather than a single-material system, and doing so at a documented 82% end-of-life-material rate, a useful benchmark figure to cite against this project's own material sourcing ambitions.

## How Fibre Fits the Existing System

| | Metal | GFRC | Wood | **Fibre (new)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Best structural role | Frame/tension | Thin-shell skin | Structural sections + infill | Tension elements, woven skin, compression-reversed modules |
| Processing infrastructure | Scrapyards, fabricators | Weak — fabricator-dependent | Growing (CLT offcuts) | **Established** — commercial net-to-textile recycling already exists (REFINVERSE, MARINYLON) |
| Biomimetic fit | Indirect (form follows curvature) | Indirect (casting geometry) | Indirect (grading logic) | **Direct** — Recycled Rope Pavilion is explicitly biomimetic, tension-to-compression reversal |
| Precedent strength | Strong (Nest/Zephyr/Orb) | Strong (Thinking Space) | Strong (EPFL/Sanchez) | Strong (Recycled Rope Pavilion, ¡Qué Faena(r)!) |

**Chain 10 — Reclaimed rope → biomimetic tension-to-compression reversal → bundled/coiled brick modules → Precedent: Recycled Rope Pavilion.** Strategy: rather than using a material only in the structural role it's naturally suited to (rope in tension), study its behaviour under a different loading condition and design a module shape specifically to make that reversal work — directly following the "natural mimicry as behaviour, not shape" principle already established in `07_Computational_Design/BIOMORPHIC_GROWTH_ALGORITHMS.md`.

**Chain 11 — Reclaimed fishing net → existing commercial fibre-recycling infrastructure → woven/knitted skin or textile panel → Precedent: ¡Qué Faena(r)!.** Strategy: leverage an already-established recycling supply chain (net-to-textile-yarn) rather than inventing a new processing method from scratch — the lowest-sourcing-risk fibre option researched this semester.

## Recommendation for This Project

Fibre is best positioned as a **skin/infill/tension layer**, not a primary structural material — complementing metal's frame role and GFRC's shell role, the same function-hierarchy logic already established via the Blue Ocean Dome and Concept D (Layered Hierarchy). Reclaimed fishing net is the strongest sourcing bet given its existing commercial processing pathway; reclaimed rope offers the most direct biomimetic design story if a compression-module direction is pursued; reclaimed textile/denim is best reserved for a genuinely secondary role (interior lining, acoustic/thermal infill) given its higher relative cost and mixed carbon case.
