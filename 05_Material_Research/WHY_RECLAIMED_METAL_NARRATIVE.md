# Why Reclaimed Metal Fits This Project — A Narrative

## The Starting Problem

The brief for this pavilion begins with a computational idea, not a material one: a differential-growth algorithm that generates form the way living systems do — through repeated, local, simple rules rather than a single top-down drawing. Repulsion, cohesion, insertion. The result is never uniform. Every agent in the simulation responds to its own immediate neighbours, so the network that emerges is full of small variations — panels that are almost, but never quite, the same size; nodes that meet at slightly different angles depending on where they sit in the form. That is the whole point of growing a structure instead of drawing one.

The question this raises immediately is a practical one: what do you actually build that network *out of*? Most new construction materials — dimensional lumber, standard steel sections, off-the-shelf sheet goods — are manufactured to be identical to each other. A growth algorithm's output is the opposite of that. It needs a material that doesn't mind being cut, joined, and assembled slightly differently every single time.

Reclaimed metal turns out to answer that question almost by accident.

## Abundance Without the Wrong Kind of Uniformity

Steel is, by a wide margin, the most recycled material on earth — roughly 1,085 million tonnes of it recycled worldwide every year, with 99% of structural steel eventually finding its way back into use. That statistic usually gets used to argue that steel is *sustainable*. For this project it means something more specific: steel is not a scarce material to go hunting for. It is sitting in scrapyards, demolition sites, and roofing strip-outs constantly, in genuinely large volume. The scarcity in reclaimed metal was never really about quantity — it's about *geometry*. Every reclaimed I-beam, every offcut of roofing sheet, every length of scrap tube arrives with its own history: a slightly different length, a bend nobody planned, a rust pattern that tells you where it used to be bolted to something else. That is exactly the kind of material a growth algorithm's uneven, non-repeating network was always going to need. Rather than fighting reclaimed metal's inconsistency, or spending months sorting it into artificially uniform batches, the computational logic of the project can simply be allowed to respond to whatever irregularity the material already has. The two problems — an irregular structural network, and an irregular material stream — cancel each other out instead of compounding.

## What the Material Itself Can Actually Do

This isn't only a convenient metaphor. Reclaimed structural steel keeps its full mechanical performance after reuse in a way that reclaimed concrete or degraded timber simply cannot — a steel beam that carried real loads in a real building for decades has already proven, in practice, what it's capable of. That means the growth network's structural members don't need to be over-specified out of caution the way a completely untested reclaimed material might demand. And unlike concrete, metal doesn't need to be poured into a shape and then be stuck with it — it can be cut, bent, and rejoined an almost unlimited number of times without losing its basic properties, which matters enormously for a project that's still testing four different pavilion directions and hasn't committed to a final geometry yet.

Thin metal sheet in particular turned out to be the real discovery in the material research. It isn't just abundant — abundant in a specific, useful way, arriving from roofing strip-outs and demolition close to panel scale already, needing very little processing before it can become architecture. And when it's thin enough, curvature itself becomes the structure. That single fact is what connects reclaimed metal directly back to the growth algorithm: the same double-curved, emergent surface a differential-growth simulation naturally wants to produce is precisely the geometry that lets a 2–3mm sheet of metal support real loads without a separate frame behind it. The material and the computational method aren't just compatible — they were, without anyone planning it that way, solving the same problem from two different directions.

## Proof, Not Just Theory

None of this would carry much weight if it were only a plausible argument. What makes reclaimed metal genuinely convincing for this project is that it's already been built, at real scale, more than once. The Thin-Shell Nest Pavilion in China spans eleven metres using two layers of 2.5mm plate — a span-to-thickness ratio of 4,400 to 1 — held together entirely with bolts, no welding at all, specifically so the whole structure could be trusted to come apart cleanly. It was. The pavilion was dismantled and rebuilt at a different site two years after it first went up, which is the rare case of a "design for disassembly" claim actually being tested rather than just asserted. Marc Fornes' studio has pushed the same underlying idea — thin aluminium, computationally segmented into interlocking stripes, riveted rather than welded — from a forty-eight-foot campus pavilion up to a ten-metre sculptural shell for Google with over two hundred thousand rivets in it, proving the method scales rather than only working as a small demonstration piece. In every one of these projects, the structure and the skin are the same layer. There is no frame hiding underneath doing the real work while the metal just decorates the surface. The thinness is the strength, and the strength comes directly from letting the geometry curve the way a growth simulation wants it to curve anyway.

## Fabrication That Matches the Material's Honesty

Reclaimed metal also happens to suit fabrication methods that are achievable without waiting for the digital fabrication facilities that only open once the Hong Kong phase begins. Folding and bending thin sheet by hand, riveting panels together, bolting nodes so they can be undone later — none of it requires a robot arm or a CNC mill to get started. Welding stays available for the handful of places in the structure where permanence is actually the right choice, while the rest of the network can be left genuinely reversible, true to the studio's whole premise that reclaimed material should be able to be reclaimed again. That flexibility between permanent and demountable, chosen deliberately per joint rather than applied uniformly, is itself a small expression of the same "grow, don't just assemble" thinking that started this whole line of research.

## The Honest Summary

Reclaimed metal fits this project not because it's the most poetic or the most obviously "sustainable-sounding" material on the list, but because its actual physical behaviour — abundant, irregular, structurally proven, capable of carrying load through curvature alone once it's thin enough — lines up with what a growth-algorithm-generated structure genuinely needs, more precisely than any of the other materials researched this semester. GFRC needed a fibre substitution just to solve its sourcing problem. Wood's best category, engineered offcuts, is promising but still depends on finding a fabricator relationship that doesn't yet exist. Metal's advantages were already sitting in scrapyards, proven at pavilion scale by more than one built project, and available to start working with by hand this week.
